What Do We Mean by Emotional Maturity?

Knowing and doing are not the same

These days, many of us use the term emotional maturity without understanding its meaning. We ask people to be emotionally mature, but a lot of times we ourselves don’t know how to be.

Your emotions are not always mutually exclusive; you experience them in combination. While preparing for an interview, you might be stressed, but also highly motivated by the reward that you will get at the end of the process.

Literally, it is a complex mixture of electrical signals and chemical reactions inside our body. If you are very curious about this, then please go ahead and check out the annexure at the end of this blog; it explains our brains’ chemistry in concise and simple terms.

But now I will focus on what people mean by the term emotional maturity in our daily lives. A lot of us might ignore our emotions, which can greatly influence how we approach and live our lives.

A simple emotion can cause a tragedy; it can fail a marriage; it can make someone a murderer; it can motivate people to wage wars, and many such things.

It can also do wonders: it can make them build monuments for their loved ones; it can make our days blissful or miserable without moving a brick, and many such things.

So if it can do both, it is in our hands to decide the outcomes. But it’s not as simple as I say it.

Two Aspects of the Brain

You see, based on what we know so far, your brain has two aspects that are strongly interconnected with one another both physically and functionally. One is your emotional brain, and the other is the cognitive brain.

If some danger approaches you, then the emotional brain acts quickly and prepares your body to defend yourself as soon as possible.

This is the part that makes you cry unconsciously when you feel physical or mental pain. This is the part that triggers you to shout when there is a small disagreement with your partner or parents.

The same part makes you jump out of the road when a car is about to crash into you.

Like this, your emotional brain can trigger false alarms sometimes. Instead of a real car crash, it could be just a dream. But your emotional brain doesn’t know the difference.

The cognitive brain (Prefrontal Cortex) is capable of thinking, analysing, and making rational decisions.

Together with the help of some other parts of your brain, it will identify these false alarms and suppress your emotional reaction. If you act before this happens, then you will be called emotionally immature.

I am not saying the cognitive brain will always make correct rational decisions. These decisions themselves are constrained by everyone’s individual knowledge and awareness.

But at least you won’t be reacting to defend yourself against something that is actually not a threat but a mere misunderstanding.

Don’t Expect it All the Time

So does it mean being emotionally mature is just a matter of practice, and we are ignorant enough not to do that?

I think the first step of being emotionally mature is not expecting everyone to be emotionally mature all the time. Your emotional brain activates unconsciously; sometimes you might react to events before your rational mind can take control of it.

No one can be emotionally mature at all times. Sometimes people break; they may make mistakes and do crazy stuff.

So if you really love them and want to keep them around, then instead of judging, try to help. You can correct their mistake later; first, be there for them. Give them time to regain their rational thinking.

But when you are the one in that emotional situation, the first step is identifying your emotion. You should try not to react immediately.

Instead, calm yourself down and process your thoughts. You may use techniques like slow breathing, exercise, or focusing on something that you like, which are proven to help you calm down.

Reach out to someone whom you can trust. Tell them how you feel, and get their opinion and advice. If you can’t find anyone like that yet, don’t worry.

Sit down somewhere and write about how and what you are feeling. I think this is one of the best ways to process your thoughts. You can do any kind of craft, like drawing, sewing, painting, etc., but writing is the simplest and most basic thing anyone can do to achieve that.

The Tricky Part

I am asking you to pause after any emotional breaking point, think about it, and make a decision. But be careful there.

This might itself become a problem sometimes; you should not spend all the time overthinking and over-analysing your emotions. Thinking too much without any improvement is also not healthy.

Suppose you had a fight with your partner; first, you should pause and calm yourself down. Then think about it, conclude, and communicate it.

But if you start thinking: what if he or she leaves me, what if I hurt them, what if the other person actually doesn’t like me? If you start thinking about all the negative what-ifs, then that itself will be considered a threat or uncertainty by your brain and intensify the situation recursively.

Instead, monitor your thinking to see whether it is actually helping you or not; if not, just don’t go there yet. Focus on something else or seek help from other people.

You should know how to identify your emotions, analyse what is going on, and decide how to react in a responsible way. In a nutshell, this is emotional intelligence. You will gain this intelligence by regularly practising and learning about your emotions.

Knowing is Not the Same as Doing

When you think about it, you can be emotionally intelligent, but not emotionally mature. The two are not the same thing. Knowing and doing are not the same.

You might know every emotion and how to respond to it in a responsible way, but when it’s time to use your skills, you will just react without thinking because that’s easy and convenient.

Suppose you take this situation: your daughter asks you to let her go to a movie tonight with her friends. You initially disagree with her, but she convinces you.

She comes home in the middle of the night and tells you that there was a guy who harassed her at the theatre. Your emotional brain might tell you to scold her. You might want to tell her that you told her so.

But if you wait and think about it for a few minutes, you will see that she did nothing wrong. It’s not her fault someone behaved wrongly. If you pause and think, then you might realise that all you want is for her to be safe, and yelling at her is not the way to do that.

First, you can check whether she is okay. If she has any issues, then figure out a way to fix them first. Then you can take action against the perpetrator.

This is not as easy as I say, because when you already have emotional conflict with someone, your brain might naturally try to attack them or defend yourself. So you have to calm yourself down and decide what to do next.

This is what people actually mean when they ask you to be emotionally mature: every time you are in such a situation, pause for a moment, calm yourself down, identify your emotion, and react to it responsibly. How to be responsible is a topic for another day. If you practise this consistently, at some point it will become your second nature.

How does our emotional brain work?

In the simplest terms, there are three parts to this.

First, electrical signals send sensory information, like what we see or hear, to the sub-parts of the brain as fast as possible. The neuron cells use electrical signals to communicate inside a cell.

To bridge the actual physical gap between adjacent neuron cells, our brain uses different neurotransmitters like dopamine, serotonin, etc.

After that, to make our body respond to these emotions, hormones are released into our bloodstream.

Adrenaline, cortisol, oxytocin, and other hormones are poured into our bloodstream, which may happen for seconds to hours based on different factors.

Also, some of these chemicals, like oxytocin, can work as both a hormone and a neurotransmitter.

How is fear or threat processed?

When your body detects danger, like a person screaming or a vehicle crashing, you suddenly become so focused and alert.

There is a part of our brain called the Amygdala, which is the main hub that works with fear or threat. Based on the sensory signals from our body, it immediately triggers an alarm to the body and also sends electrical signals to both the Hypothalamus and Hippocampus.

The Hypothalamus initiates the release of Adrenaline and Cortisol in your body from the adrenal glands, preparing you to defend yourself.

Adrenaline release is triggered by the sympathetic nervous system, which connects the brain to other organs like the heart, lungs, and the digestive system. Cortisol is released in a different and slower process.

The Hippocampus helps to retrieve our memories relating to this situation and sensory feelings and provides context to the Cingulate Gyrus and Prefrontal Cortex.

Also, based on the intensity of the emotion, it would be recorded by the Hippocampus for future reference, which is driven by the Amygdala.

Suppose you are in a movie theatre watching an action movie; here a car accident or a person screaming on the screen will seem safe and fun rather than something to be serious about.

So the Cingulate Gyrus will identify that as a conflicting reaction to an emotion.

Then your Prefrontal Cortex will analyse the situation and decide whether the situation is actually a threat. This is done based on your memory, context, situation, expectations, and other such factors.

If not, then the Prefrontal Cortex tries to turn down the Amygdala alarm. Then the Hypothalamus uses the parasympathetic nervous system to slow down the heart rate. Also, the stress hormones and related brain activities are reversed.

Similarly, functions of different brain regions, sensory information, memory, context, and other bodily reactions work together in an interconnected manner to construct our complex emotions.

Some important chemical components

Each of these chemicals will do more than what I describe here. For simplicity, I kept what is relevant for our topic.

  • Adrenaline (Hormone): Triggers an immediate response across the body, like increasing the heart rate and breathing.
  • Cortisol (Hormone): Maintains sustained energy and stress management across the body from hours to days.
  • Dopamine (Neurotransmitter): Drives motivation, focus, and anticipation for some reward.
  • Serotonin (Neurotransmitter): Maintains our mood stability and calmness. Around ninety percent of Serotonin is produced in the gut, which influences digestion. Brain serotonin influences mood, sleep, and appetite. The gut serotonin pool will not be useful in the brain, and vice versa.
  • Glutamate & GABA (Neurotransmitters): These are the excitatory and inhibitory transmitters of the brain. Both of these chemicals help increase or decrease brain alertness and activity based on the situation.
  • Oxytocin (Hormone & Neurotransmitter): It acts as both a hormone in the blood and a neurotransmitter inside the brain. It influences social recognition, attachment, reproduction, and stress response.
  • Endorphins (Hormone & Neuropeptides): They act as both neuropeptides and hormones. Neuropeptides are like Neurotransmitters but slower, last longer, and cover a wider area. They help manage physical/emotional pain, stress response, reward, and experience pleasure or excitement.
  • Endocannabinoids (Retrograde Neurotransmitters): The prefix retrograde signifies that this communicates backwards between neurons. The receiving neuron will release it back to the sending neuron to restrain it from sending the signal anymore. In this specific case, it helps with ease and relaxation.
  • Acetylcholine (Neurotransmitter): Released by the vagus nerve to lower your heart rate. It is also used in the brain for functions including attention and learning.
