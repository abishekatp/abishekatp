How many of you guys are overthinkers here? If you are someone who constantly overthinks and worries about the same thing for days, weeks, or months, then it’s time to get out of that hell.

I was a big-time overthinker myself. For some time now, I have been trying to get out of that eternal and vicious cycle of overthinking that does no good for me or anyone else around me.

Thinking is actually good; it helped me come this far in my life. It actually helped me many times in my life to make decisions that improved my life.

But how can that same thing do any harm to me? Because of this confusion, I didn’t focus much on my overthinking behaviour until it became really exhausting.

To overcome this, I have to systematically redirect my energy by filtering out thoughts that are unnecessary and unproductive.

The following are the things that have helped me to cope with this overthinking behaviour. I will try to explain each step with an example.

### **Overthinking vs Productive Thinking**

The first step is to differentiate between overthinking and productive thinking.

Productive thinking usually helps me make decisions, or at least leads me closer to some conclusion about the situation. Sometimes that conclusion helps me move on to the next important thing instead of hanging on to the same thought.

Sometimes I can take an action to change things that I am worrying about and move on to the next thing.

But when I am overthinking, it won’t give me any conclusion; instead, I will endlessly worry about the same thing without any improvement.

Sometimes I might have a conclusion, for example, when I am regretting some past event, but I might not be able to take any action to change that situation.

A few years back, I had just joined my new job, and one such event happened in my workplace. I missed a call from a colleague. Later, when I pinged or called him, he was not responding.

I started worrying about that situation. That was a clear example of overthinking, and we will see why that was so.

### **Identify Overthinking**

A lot of this overthinking starts with the question “What if?” It could be either about past events or about future events, but it would always start with this question.

For example, what if he thinks my message was rude? What if I didn’t try enough to achieve that goal? What if I study this subject and it becomes obsolete in the future?

These questions are genuinely good for getting a new perspective. They are the same questions I would ask while doing actual critical thinking about something important.

But the problem doesn’t originate from the question, but rather from either the arguments that I use to answer those questions or my inability to accept reality sometimes. The latter case I will address later; let’s first focus on the assumptions part.

Arguments can have facts, assumptions and supporting statements. One clear way to identify overthinking is to notice when the majority of my arguments are assumptions.

For example, I assumed that my colleague would be disappointed because I didn’t pick up his call. That assumption was the basis for all of my worries.

### **Handle the Assumptions**

Overthinking will worsen when I don’t know all the facts about the situation. In this case, I will have a lot more space to fill in with my own imagination.

Since I will have different ways to make assumptions, this will only create many possible conclusions. When I have many such conclusions, it will overwhelm me with not knowing what will actually happen.

So, as a first step to avoid overthinking, I need to separate all my assumptions from my arguments. I have to consider the weight of those assumptions when making decisions.

Assumptions are used even in critical thinking to draw conclusions, but when I have more assumptions than facts, then that argument becomes useless.

Instead of trying to resolve my thoughts using assumptions, I can just leave them alone until I get more facts about that situation.

But there is also a problem with collecting facts for every little bout of overthinking, especially when I have to depend on another person to confirm those facts.

For example, I went and apologised to my colleague and clarified my assumption with him. After a week, I got a response from him telling me there was nothing to worry about.

If I did that every time I overthink something, then that would only imply that either I don’t trust him, or I don’t trust my own self-worth. So I need to ignore my assumptions and go with trusting people.

### **Learn to Live with Uncertainty**

When I accept that a particular thought is overthinking and try to ignore that thought, then I will have to face the uncertainty that comes with that.

In my example, I could forget the assumption that he might be angry with me and just wait for the reply. But this would put me in an uncertain situation. I would have to constantly worry about whether he would reply or not.

But later I came to know that, regardless of whether I sent him that apology or got confirmation, he wouldn’t have been able to reply earlier, because he had just lost his mother that week.

So that uncertainty would have continued for some time.

So, instead of quickly judging people, I started to observe them over some period, like a few days, weeks or even months. If I still feel like there is something wrong, then I will discuss it with that person.

Even when I have a discussion, at some point I will have to trust that the other person is telling the truth. Without that trust, the uncertainty will continue to grow.

Later, I understood that wanting to be on the good side of everyone was a reason for this overthinking. Being uncertain about where I stood with everyone made me anxious, which I needed to address.

So, the best way to approach this is to accept the uncertainty of not having answers to all of my thoughts and be able to live with that.

### **Acceptance**

I talked about the inability to accept reality as one more reason for overthinking. For some people, there might be a core reason behind a specific pattern of overthinking.

For example, after my schooling, I wanted to become a doctor, but I didn’t become one because of many situations at that time.

Instead, I blamed myself for not putting enough effort into making the right decision about my higher education. It got me into an overthinking cycle of self-blame.

My strong belief that I could achieve anything if I put enough thought and effort into it was a core motivation for my overthinking, and it was not a healthy way to approach life.

I needed some time to understand and accept that I had made that decision with all the knowledge available at that time in my best interest.

Again, I would ask myself, “Could I have explored more knowledge to make that decision?”

To fully overcome that situation, I needed to accept the fact that there would be a lot of random events that would influence my life decisions. So whatever path I took, there would always be another path that I would regret not taking.

Also, I could not keep on worrying about past events, which are not going to help me in any way to build a better future. Instead, I could learn from them and move on to the next best thing that could improve my life.

The same thing goes for my colleague example also. I should accept the fact that he might have something going on in his own life, and I shouldn’t overthink each of his reactions.

So, to stop overthinking, I would also need some level of self-acceptance and acceptance of the randomness of this world.

### **Focus on What’s Important Now**

After identifying such thoughts, I should become a spectator in my own mind and tell myself that my mind is overthinking that situation without any improvement.

I should refocus on something that needs my attention; there are plenty of things that could improve my life.

If I have nothing to do, that’s where hobbies might help. I can go for a run, walk, go to the gym, read, play, write, etc. – anything other than staying in that overthinking cycle forever.

Recently, whenever someone misses my call or doesn’t react to my action, I have been trying not to overanalyse it. I watch my thoughts, spot the assumptions, and refocus myself on something else that matters.

But some of these thoughts are strongly entangled with my emotions. So every time I suppress them, they try to gain my attention again even more strongly.

To manage this situation, I started allocating separate time to think about these things.

Whenever I enter into some overthinking thoughts, I tell myself the following: “I am going to focus on my work for now and think about this later in the evening at a specific time.”

Even at that time, I allocate a few minutes, based on the importance of that thought.

This minimises the time wasted on unnecessary thoughts. A lot of times, when given enough time, I might come out of my emotional state and be able to think more clearly about that situation.

### **The Conclusion**

We can’t get over this overthinking overnight. For each one of us, it might take a different amount of time and effort. We should patiently wait and consciously train our minds not to overthink.

Still, there will be days when we will do things because of overthinking, and we might regret them later. But don’t overthink that regret again. That’s the irony of overthinking; it gets worse so quickly by amplifying itself.

Instead, follow the above process consistently, and it will get better every time we practise it. Let thinking do what it actually has to do.
